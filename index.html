Wills vs. Trusts in Missouri: Which One Do You Need?

If you have started thinking about an estate plan, you have probably run into the big question early: should you have a will, a trust, or both? There is no one right answer for every family. The best fit depends on what you own, who you want to provide for, and how much you want your family to deal with the probate court after you are gone.

This page walks through how each tool works under Missouri law, where each one shines, and the questions David Koonce asks St. Louis families when helping them decide.

What a will does

A will is a written set of instructions for what happens after your death. In Missouri, a will can:

  • Name the people or charities who receive your property
  • Appoint a personal representative to settle your estate
  • Name a guardian for your minor children
  • Create a trust for a child or another beneficiary

To be valid in Missouri, a will must be in writing, signed by you, and signed by two competent witnesses (RSMo 474.320). Missouri does not accept unwitnessed handwritten wills. Adding a self-proving affidavit signed before a notary can make the will easier to admit to probate later.

A will has to go through probate to work

This is the part many people miss. Under Missouri law, a will has to be presented to and admitted by the probate court to be effective (RSMo 473.050). In general, it must be presented within one year after death. So a will does not avoid probate. It tells the court and your personal representative what you want, and the court process carries it out.

For small estates, Missouri offers a shortcut. If the estate is worth $40,000 or less after liens and debts, and at least 30 days have passed since the death, family members may be able to use a small estate affidavit instead of a full probate case (RSMo 473.097).

What a revocable living trust does

A revocable living trust is a legal arrangement you create during your lifetime. You transfer ("fund") your assets into the trust, and you usually serve as your own trustee. You keep full control: you can buy, sell, and use the property just as before, and you can change or cancel the trust at any time.

When you pass away, or if you can no longer manage your affairs, the successor trustee you chose steps in and follows your written instructions. Because the trust, not you personally, holds title to the assets, those assets pass without a probate case.

The funding step matters

A trust only controls what is titled in its name. If you sign a trust but never retitle your house or accounts, those assets may still end up in probate. That is why a trust is almost always paired with a pour-over will, which catches anything left outside the trust and moves it in. Funding is part of the work, not an afterthought. Wills vs. trusts at a glance

Will Revocable Living Trust
Probate A will goes through probate. Assets properly titled in a trust do not.
Privacy A probated will becomes part of the court file. A trust is generally not filed with the court.
Incapacity A will only takes effect at death. A trust lets your successor trustee manage trust assets if you become unable to, without a court-ordered conservatorship. (A durable power of attorney covers other assets and is a good idea either way.)
Guardians for children Only a will names a guardian for minor children. If you have young kids, you need a will even if you also have a trust.
Upfront work and cost A will is usually simpler and less expensive to prepare. [CONFIRM WITH DAVID: fee ranges or flat fees for a will-based plan vs. a trust-based plan, if he wants them mentioned] A trust takes more work up front, including funding, but can save your family time and court costs later.
Creditor protection Neither a will nor a revocable trust protects your assets from your own creditors or long-term care costs while you are alive. Because you keep control of a revocable trust, its assets are still treated as yours.

When a will may be enough

A will-based plan can make sense when:

  • Your estate is modest and fairly simple
  • Most of your assets already pass by beneficiary designation, such as retirement accounts, life insurance, and payable-on-death bank accounts
  • You are comfortable with your family going through probate, or the estate may qualify for the small estate procedure
  • Your main goal right now is naming a guardian for young children

Missouri also lets you add transfer-on-death beneficiaries to many assets, including real estate through a beneficiary deed (RSMo 461.025). For some families, a will plus well-coordinated beneficiary designations keeps most assets out of probate without a trust. The catch is that beneficiary designations need to be kept up to date, and they do not handle incapacity or staggered distributions the way a trust can.

When a trust is often the better fit

A revocable living trust is worth a close look when:

  • You own a home or other real estate, especially property in more than one state
  • You want to keep your affairs private
  • You want to avoid probate time and cost for your family
  • You have children or grandchildren who are not ready to receive a lump sum
  • You have a blended family and want clear, detailed instructions
  • You own a business and want a plan for who manages it if something happens to you
  • You want a ready plan in place if you become ill or incapacitated

What about taxes?

Missouri does not have a state estate tax or inheritance tax. At the federal level, the estate tax basic exclusion amount for people who die in 2026 is $15,000,000 per person, so most families will not owe federal estate tax. Even so, taxes still matter in estate planning. Income tax on inherited retirement accounts and the tax basis of inherited property can make a real difference to what your family keeps.

Frequently asked questions

Do I still need a will if I have a living trust?

Yes. A pour-over will catches any assets left outside your trust and moves them into it. It is also where you name a guardian for minor children.

Does a living trust help me avoid estate taxes?

A standard revocable living trust does not by itself reduce federal estate tax. Its main benefits are avoiding probate, privacy, and incapacity planning. Missouri has no state estate tax, and the 2026 federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person. Larger estates may use other planning tools.

Is a trust only for wealthy people?

No. Many middle-income families use a revocable living trust to keep their home out of probate, plan for incapacity, or control how and when children receive an inheritance.

What happens if I die without a will or trust in Missouri?

Your property passes under Missouri's intestacy rules, which set out who inherits based on family relationships. The court appoints someone to manage your estate and decides who cares for minor children.

Can I change my will or trust later?

Yes. You can update a will or a revocable trust at any time while you have capacity. It is a good idea to review your plan after major life changes such as marriage, divorce, a birth, a death in the family, a move, or a big change in assets.
